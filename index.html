Getting around

About the shape: nobody has seen the Milky Way from outside, so this is a model. The central bar and the spiral arms follow measured distances to young star-forming regions on our side of the galaxy (Reid et al. 2019) and continue round the far side as smooth spirals. Distances are to scale; the galaxy turns far faster than the real one: the Sun takes about 200 million years to go round once.

About You Are Here

A 3D journey from the Milky Way to Earth, by Joseph Florendo. Sources · Credits and licences

Computed

  • Where the Sun, the Moon, the planets and Pluto are on the clock's date, from Astronomy Engine (accurate to within about 1 arcminute).
  • Earth's spin and tilt, day and night, the Moon's phase, and real solar and lunar eclipses.
  • Every planet's tilt and spin, from the IAU's rotation models (so Venus, Uranus and Pluto spin backwards).
  • The galactic plane meeting the solar system's plane at about 60°.

Illustrative

  • The galaxy's shape is a model: nobody has seen the Milky Way from outside. Its spin is sped up enormously.
  • Sizes and distances are not to scale unless the note under the clock says so: planets are enlarged, the Sun is shrunk and distances are squeezed.
  • In the eclipse view the Sun and the Moon are drawn larger than they look, both by the same amount.
  • Shooting stars are decoration. City lights are today's, whatever the date.
  • Surface colours come from processed maps; Uranus and Neptune are nearly featureless, and Pluto's colour is a tint on a grey map whose southern part was never photographed.
